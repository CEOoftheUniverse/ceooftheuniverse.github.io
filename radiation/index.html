Short, kind version first, because you asked a real question about your own body: lower damaging UV is genuinely better for your skin and your eyes — the two things you flagged. Lower ambient (background) radiation matters too, and the one that actually varies by home is radon. But "as little sun as possible" is not the healthiest target — a little gentle sun carries real benefits. And your dislike of cream is completely valid; you don't need cream to be sun-safe. Here is the evidence, softly laid out. 💛

1 · How much does UV actually harm? UV

UV is the sunlight band that damages living tissue. The honest magnitude:

  • Skin cancer. Melanoma is only ~3% of skin-cancer cases but causes >75% of skin-cancer deaths, and UV is its most preventable cause; non-melanoma skin cancers are far more common and also UV-driven (EPA, PMC 2024 review).
  • Photoaging. UVA reaches deeper and does most of the visible aging — wrinkles, collagen loss, broken capillaries, early freckling (Johns Hopkins).
  • Your eyes (you specifically mentioned eyes). UV raises the risk of certain cataracts, plus pterygium ("surfer's eye"), photokeratitis (sunburn of the cornea), and is linked to ocular surface damage (EPA, cataract–melanoma study 2024). Eyes have no "tan" defense — protection is the only lever.
  • Immune effect. High UV locally suppresses skin immune response (IARC).

What makes UV high in a place: latitude, altitude (~+10% per 1,000 m), ozone thinness, cloud, and surface reflection (snow nearly doubles it). The planet's most extreme UV is the high, low-ozone Andes/Atacama — a UV index of 43.3 was recorded on Bolivia's Licancabur (LiveScience). This is exactly why, later, the Swiss Alps and the NZ/Tasmania spots don't score as "clean" as their fresh air suggests.

2 · Ambient / background radiation at habitat level ionizing

Separate from UV, we all absorb a little natural ionizing radiation every year. The global average is about 2.4 mSv/year (UNSCEAR 2008; a 2026 UN re-estimate puts it near 3.0 mSv on better data, not more actual radiation — UNIS 2026). Roughly:

The one worth caring about is radon: a radioactive gas that seeps from the ground into homes. It is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking — and the first among people who never smoked; WHO attributes a meaningful share of lung cancers to it, and each +100 Bq/m³ indoors raises lung-cancer risk ~16% (NCI, EPA, global trend 2025). It tracks geology: highest in granitic and karst rock (Central Alps crystalline rock, the Dinaric karst through Slovenia, Corsica/Sardinia granite), lower over young basalt (Iceland, Jeju) and sedimentary coasts (EEA). The good news, in your low-tox spirit: radon is cheap to test and usually fixable by sealing + ventilation — it's a property-level fix, not a destiny.

3 · Sunscreen — a real fix, with real (and valid) downsides cream

Does it work?

Yes — sunscreen measurably lowers squamous-cell carcinoma and photoaging, and the evidence supports a melanoma benefit with good application. That part isn't in doubt.

The honest downsides

  • Chemical filters get into your blood. An FDA/JAMA study found common chemical UV filters (oxybenzone highest) are absorbed above FDA's safety-testing threshold after normal use (FDA/JAMA, summary). Important honesty: absorption ≠ proven harm — the same authors said keep using sunscreen. But if it bothers you, there's a clean answer below.
  • Only the mineral two are "generally recognized as safe" by the FDA — zinc oxide and titanium dioxide. The chemical filters are "needs more data," not cleared (FDA 2019). So your instinct to avoid the typical cream isn't unreasonable.
  • Your eye irritation is real. Fragrance and volatile chemical filters genuinely sting eyes, and sunscreen running in sweat is a classic complaint — not imagined.

"The skin can't breathe" — gently corrected, but your feeling is valid

Biologically, skin doesn't "breathe" like lungs: the outer layer is dead keratin, and the living layers get their oxygen from blood, not from air — so even full occlusion doesn't suffocate skin (Lab Muffin, science-based, Skincare.com). But the discomfort you feel is real and has real causes — occlusion traps heat and sweat (skin's cooling is what actually matters), and heavy or fragranced formulas feel awful and clog. So the fix isn't "let skin breathe" — it's lighter, cleaner protection that doesn't trap heat or run into your eyes. 🌸

4 · The honest tradeoff — is less sun simply better?

No — and this is the part most sun-scare advice gets wrong. In the long-running Melanoma in Southern Sweden cohort, women who avoided the sun had roughly double the all-cause mortality of the highest-sun group, the excess mainly cardiovascular — sun avoidance behaved like a risk factor on the scale of smoking (Lindqvist 2014, 2016). More sun meant more skin cancer but not shorter life.

Caveats, kept honest: it's observational (not proof of cause), it's a cloudy low-UV Nordic population (so "more sun" there is modest), and the authors note vitamin D may just be a marker of sun, with the real benefit perhaps via other pathways (nitric oxide, mood/circadian). So don't read it as "sunbathe freely in Bali."

The balanced target, in your own framing. You taught the atlas a rule: "time spent is not time lost — count only the harm." Sun is the same. The goal isn't zero UV; it's gentle, non-burning exposure (short daily minutes, outside the ~10:00–16:00 peak) with eyes and face protected, in a place whose baseline UV is moderate rather than extreme. That keeps the mood/vitamin-D/cardiovascular upside while cutting the cancer-and-cataract downside. A very-low-UV place (Reykjavík) is "cleanest" on paper but you'd then want to seek a little sun or top up vitamin D; a very-high-UV place (Bali, Mexico City, NZ) means you can't skip protection.

5 · Your cream-free sun toolkit 🧴🚫 → 🧢🕶️

Because you dislike cream, here's protection that fits your low-tox, natural-materials, barefoot-living style — no greasy film, nothing in your eyes:

  • UPF clothing (long, light, breathable linen/technical weave) — the single best no-cream shield; it never runs or stings.
  • Wide-brim hat — shades face + eyes together.
  • Wraparound UV400 sunglasses — directly protects the eyes you're worried about (cataract/pterygium prevention); wraparound beats flat lenses for side-entry light.
  • Shade + timing — the free one: plan outdoor time before 10:00 / after 16:00; the UV index roughly halves outside the peak.
  • Lower-UV base — choosing a moderate-UV home (the atlas column) does the work passively, every day, with zero product.
  • UV window/car film — cuts indoor/driving UVA (a real, often-missed dose).
  • If any cream at all — a fragrance-free mineral (zinc oxide) stick on cheekbones/nose only, kept away from the eye area: no fumes, no run-off, FDA's clean category.
  • Radon — a one-time home test; if high, ventilate/seal. Fits the same "clean environment" instinct.
